1733
John Kay · The Flying Shuttle

Kay built a faster shuttle and barely escaped England with his life. Workers petitioned the King, wrecked looms, and drove him out. He died in poverty in France, never having profited from his own invention.

They were not wrong to be angry. Some were displaced, and some of those workers never found other employment. But weaving as an occupation survived the flying shuttle. The industry grew beyond anything Kay's contemporaries could have imagined, and it eventually employed more people. But not the same people.

1801
Jacquard · The Programmable Loom

Jacquard's loom read instructions from punched cards and could weave any pattern imaginable. Workers in Lyon tried to destroy it and him. Attempts were made on his life.

Weavers who had spent years mastering intricate patterns by hand found their skill suddenly worthless. Some were never reemployed. But the fabrics his loom made possible created an industry larger than the one it disrupted. We have Paris fashion week because of it. His punched cards, the very mechanism of their displacement, became the template for Babbage's Analytical Engine.

1843
Ada Lovelace · The Analytical Engine

Lovelace was asked to translate a paper about Babbage's machine. She did, then added notes longer than the paper itself. Those notes contained the first algorithm ever written for a machine to execute, and something more important: the argument that a machine capable of manipulating symbols was not merely a faster calculator. It could compose music. It could do anything expressible in symbols and rules.

Lovelace wrote that it "weaves algebraic patterns just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves" — and first asked not what machines compute, but what humans and machines might create together.

2026
LOOM · The same question

The weavers who smashed Jacquard's looms were not wrong. Some of them never worked again. What they could not see, and what nobody standing inside a technological transition has ever been able to see, is the shape of what comes next: who ends up with the gains, and at whose expense.

Nearly three centuries on, that question is more urgent than the tools available to answer it. LOOM is an attempt to close that distance, not by predicting which occupations survive AI, but by measuring what changes inside every one that does.

A loom doesn't create threads.
It reveals what they can become together.

LOOM is built on a single claim: that theory, applied research, and industry impact should operate as a circuit — not a sequence. Findings from the measurement arm generate questions that the intervention platforms answer in practice. The platforms surface phenomena that the critical-policy arm scrutinises. Each feeds the next.

This isn't a traditional grant-and-publish research programme. It's a deliberately integrated architecture — designed so that three streams of work reinforce each other rather than compete for the same constrained research time.

The research opportunity

Current tools are looking at the wrong level of analysis.

The two most prominent automation risk frameworks — Routine-Cognitive-Manual task categories and Frey-Osborne automation probabilities — together explain just 3% of variation in labour demand across 250 occupations. Their theoretically distinct risk categories are statistically indistinguishable from each other.

The other 94% operates within occupations — invisible to any framework that measures at the occupation level. This isn't a gap at the margins. It's a ceiling on what the entire existing measurement infrastructure can detect.

"If automation primarily restructures tasks within occupations, the policy conversation — and the research infrastructure behind it — is systematically misdirected."

Programme architecture

Three integrated research arms

Each arm generates distinct outputs while feeding the others. The integration is the design — not a happy coincidence.

Arm 1 · Measurement

Mapping the invisible

Uses job advertisement data and skill proximity networks to detect task restructuring at sub-occupational levels — making visible what aggregate occupation statistics conceal. The core empirical engine of the programme.

Methods 9.5M job-ad corpus · Skill proximity graphs · Labour economics modelling
Arm 2 · Intervention

Live research settings

Four platforms — Ordian, Docilis, Augmenti, and Passage — form the Intervention arm's research infrastructure. The first three are designed from the ground up as research instruments, each generating observational and experimental data on human–AI task reallocation in a distinct professional context. Passage makes the Measurement arm's findings publicly navigable.

Platforms Ordian (workforce allocation) · Docilis (professional writing) · Augmenti (strategy) · Passage (public data tool)
Arm 3 · Critical Policy

Governance & its limits

Addresses what current AI governance frameworks get wrong. AI Hushing — the strategic decision by firms to conceal genuine AI use from investors and customers when disclosure would hurt them — and why human-in-the-loop oversight often fails to prevent biased outcomes.

Outputs AI Hushing theory · HITL bias critique · Policy implications

Intervention arm · Research platforms

Four platforms. Four research sites. One lab.

Each platform is designed first as a research instrument — generating observational and experimental data on human–AI task reallocation in a distinct professional context. Commercial activity is controlled and research-subordinated.

In development · targeting 2026

Ordian

AI-driven workforce allocation for universities. Matches sessional staff to teaching tasks using skill profiles and availability data.

Research role Studies how algorithmic allocation intersects with Equal Opportunity Act obligations. Designed to generate longitudinal allocation data across teaching assignments.
Target ARR (2028) A$250k–750k
2–3 university licensees
In development · targeting 2026–27

Docilis

AI writing platform for professionals whose public voice carries reputational stakes — consultants, academics, senior leaders.

Research role Studies how AI-mediated writing preserves or erodes individual voice. User interaction logs provide data on authorial decision-making under AI suggestion.
Target ARR (2028) A$150k–900k
500–2,000 paying users
Active development · MBA & enterprise

Augmenti

AI-facilitated design-thinking for innovation teams and MBA cohorts. Structures ideation, synthesis, and critique.

Research role Studies AI's role in collaborative knowledge work. Dual use in classroom (SoTL data) and enterprise settings creates matched comparison across contexts.
Target ARR (2028) A$100k–600k
5–15 enterprise & university clients
Planned · buildable now

Passage

An interactive tool for exploring the labour market data underlying LOOM — find your occupation cluster, understand what's changing inside it, and see which skill moves lead toward safer or better-paying work.

Research role Serves as the public-facing interface on the Measurement arm — making cluster assignments, skill proximity networks, and task-restructuring trends accessible to workers, policymakers, and career advisors without requiring familiarity with the underlying methodology.
Funding pathway ARC Linkage 2029
Data exists now · development independent of grant outcome

Enabling conditions

What would make this possible

Six institutional enablers that would unlock the full programme. Each is a conversation, not a demand.

01

Named CoBLTech affiliation

Formal programme visibility within CoBLTech — a named home for LOOM that signals institutional support and enables external partnership conversations with grant bodies and industry.

02

Leadership opportunities

Committee membership or stream leadership relevant to AI, labour, and future of work — builds the institutional service track record required for a strong Level C promotion case.

03

Grant partner brokering

Introductions to Jobs & Skills Australia, the Productivity Commission, and peer universities as potential ARC Linkage partners. RMIT's networks are the critical enabling input here.

04

IP & licensing support for Ordian

A conversation on RMIT's preferred IP arrangement for the Ordian platform — currently undefined. A resolved structure unlocks responsible commercial scale-up and protects both parties.

05

Job advertisement data access

Access to a comprehensive Australian job advertisement dataset (e.g. Lightcast or equivalent). The entire measurement arm — and the programme's empirical core — depends on this longitudinal data.

06

Annual showcase event

A CoBLTech-hosted annual event on AI and the future of work — convening industry, policy, and academic voices. Positions RMIT as a convener in this conversation, not simply a commentator.
