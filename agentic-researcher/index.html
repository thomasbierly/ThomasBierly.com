The Agentic Researcher

Fix one number in your data and every table, figure and result in your paper updates to match. Ask an assistant that has read your code, your data and your draft whether the Results section still says what the analysis found, and check its answer line by line.

That is what this setup gives you: the whole project in one place, from the software or survey that produces your data to the paper that reports it, with an AI agent working beside you. You stop copying output from SPSS into Word, and paper_final_v7.docx goes away. You never wonder again which version of the data made Figure 3. Every change is recorded, whether you made it or the agent did, and any of them can be undone. It runs on free software on your own computer, and setting it up takes an afternoon.

You’ll need: a computer you can give to Linux, a USB stick, an afternoon, and a Claude subscription
You won’t need: to know how to program, or to have used Linux before

Part 1Why do this at all?

Compared with SPSS, Stata and Word

SPSS, Stata and Word are good tools, and you may have used them for years. The problems come from the gaps between them. Results travel from the statistics package to the paper by copy and paste, and every paste is a chance for the paper to fall out of step with the analysis. These mistakes are easy to make and hard to spot. A spreadsheet error in an influential economics paper on public debt went unnoticed for three years. A 2016 study found that about one in five genetics papers that shared gene lists as Excel files had gene names corrupted by Excel’s automatic formatting.

In this setup the paper is built from the analysis, so there is nothing to paste. Here is how everyday tasks compare:

If you already write Stata do-files or SPSS syntax rather than clicking through menus, you’re halfway there: that is the same idea. Your agent can translate those files into Python and check that they give the same results, and Python opens your existing .sav and .dta files directly. Stata and SPSS both have Linux versions, if your field expects a particular procedure.

What it’s like to use

Most of the day is plain English. You describe what you want, the agent does the mechanical part, and you check what it did. Here are some things you might ask it, at each stage of a project:

Getting the data in

Open survey_export.sav, keep the variables in the codebook, and save a clean copy in data/.

Here’s my old SPSS syntax. Rewrite it in Python and show me that the means match.

Analysis

Regress score on sleep and stress, controlling for year, and explain the output as you would to a reviewer.

Make Figure 2 colour-blind friendly and in APA style.

Writing

Check every number in the Results section against the analysis.

Make a Word version of the paper for my co-author.

Housekeeping

Save a version called “added stress factor” and back it up to GitHub.

The PDF preview stopped updating. Can you find out why?

You stay the researcher. The agent makes changes and explains them, the review tab shows you exactly what it changed, and you can question any line. The same project structure works for a thesis, a grant proposal, a literature review or a course you teach: anything that turns data and reading into writing.

Why Linux, now

For most of the last thirty years the operating system barely mattered to research. You needed Word, a statistics package and a reference manager, and they ran on whatever you had. Agents change that, because of how they work.

An AI agent such as Claude Code doesn’t click through menus. It reads files, runs commands and writes files, the way a programmer does. Give it a computer where everything is a file and every action is a command, and it can do far more for you. That describes Linux.

Agents work where you work

Your code, data, paper and even the computer’s settings are plain text files. The agent can help with the whole project, and fix the computer when something breaks.

The same as the cluster

University computing clusters and cloud servers run Linux. What you learn on your laptop carries straight over when your analysis outgrows it.

Your data stays with you

Notebooks, databases and the grammar checker run on your own machine. Only what you send to the agent leaves it, and you decide what that is.

The honest costs. Linux asks you to learn new habits, and some things are harder. Microsoft Office runs only in the browser, some university software is Windows-only, and a few laptops have hardware Linux doesn’t fully support. The agent softens the learning curve a great deal, because you can ask it to explain or fix anything you meet. If you rely on Windows-only software, Omarchy can sit beside Windows on the same computer (see Install Omarchy).

Why Omarchy

Omarchy is a version of Linux made by David Heinemeier Hansson at 37signals, the company behind Basecamp. It makes every setup decision for you, with good taste, so the computer is ready to work the day you install it. Its tagline is the malleable OS for the age of agents, and that fits researchers right now:

  • The agents are already there. Claude Code and a dozen other agents come ready to run. On first start Omarchy asks which one you want, and when an app crashes you can click the notification to send your agent to investigate.
  • Everything is meant to be changed, by you or your agent. Omarchy teaches your agent how it works, from changing a setting to building a whole app. The setup in this guide was made that way: I described what I wanted, and Claude Code built it.
  • It’s quick to try. The installer asks a handful of questions and finishes in minutes.

We’re in a transition. Agents can now do much of the mechanical work of research, but most researchers still work in tools designed before agents existed. Omarchy is one of the first computers designed with agents in mind, and a comfortable place to learn to work alongside one. It is keyboard-first, so my setup adds a mouse-friendly layer on top: a file list you can click, a right-click menu, and a Projects menu. About six shortcuts get you started, and they’re all in the cheat sheet.

Part 2Set it up and run a project

Install Omarchy

Back up first. A normal Omarchy install erases the whole drive you install it on. Copy anything you want to keep somewhere else first. To keep Windows alongside Omarchy instead, follow Omarchy’s dual-boot guide.

1
Check what you need
A computer you’re happy to dedicate to Linux (Omarchy runs on almost anything, from recent laptops to Intel Macs and a 2011 ThinkPad), a USB stick of 8 GB or more that can be wiped, and a keyboard plugged in by cable or a 2.4 GHz dongle. A Bluetooth keyboard won’t work at the start-up password screen.
2
Download Omarchy
Go to omarchy.org and download the ISO, the installer image (about 6.5 GB).
3
Make the USB stick
On Windows or a Mac, install balenaEtcher, open it, choose Flash from file and pick the ISO, choose your USB stick as the target, and click Flash. This erases the stick.
4
Turn off Secure Boot
Restart the computer and open its BIOS settings, usually by pressing F2, F12, Del or Esc as it starts (the start-up screen often says which). Find Secure Boot and turn it off; if there’s a TPM setting, turn that off too. Save and exit.
5
Start from the USB stick
With the stick plugged in, restart and open the boot menu (often F12 or F8; on an Intel Mac, hold Option). Choose the USB stick.
6
Answer the installer’s questions
Pick your keyboard layout, then answer a few questions about you and the computer, such as your username and password, and confirm them. Then choose the drive to install on and confirm. Your password also encrypts the drive, so you’ll type it each time the computer starts. The Omarchy manual has a screenshot of each screen. The install takes a few minutes, then the computer restarts.
7
First start
Type your password to unlock the drive. Omarchy opens and invites you to choose a default agent: choose Claude to follow this guide exactly. Connect to the internet with a network cable, or click the network icon in the top bar and choose your Wi-Fi.

Omarchy starts on an empty desktop. Almost everything starts from one menu.

Install the research setup

My setup is on GitHub at thomasbierly/agentic-research. It adds what Omarchy doesn’t have for research: Quarto and Typst for papers, Zotero for references, Python notebooks, a Projects menu, and editor features for writing.

Open a terminal with Super + Enter and type these two lines, pressing Enter after each:

git clone https://github.com/thomasbierly/agentic-research ~/.local/share/agentic-research
~/.local/share/agentic-research/install.sh

It asks for your password once, to install software, then works through each part and ticks it off.

When it finishes it lists three things only you can do:

  1. Sign in to Claude Code. In the terminal, type claude, press Enter and follow the sign-in. You need a Claude subscription (Pro or Max) or an Anthropic API account. Then run the installer line again, so the project workspace can show you when Claude needs you.
  2. Add Better BibTeX to Zotero. Open Zotero from the app launcher (Super + Alt + Space). Download the .xpi file from the Better BibTeX site, then in Zotero choose Tools › Plugins, click the gear icon and choose Install Plugin From File.
  3. Connect GitHub, so your projects are backed up. In the terminal type gh auth login and follow the questions.

To update the setup later, run the installer line again.

Your first project

Every project lives in its own folder in ~/Projects and opens in its own workspace. To start one, open the Omarchy menu with Super + Space and choose Projects.

The project opens in the workspace you saw at the top of this page, with five tabs, one for each kind of work. You switch between them by clicking the tab names or with Alt + 1 to 5.

The folder has one place for each stage of the project:

FolderWhat goes in it
src/The software: a simulation, a data collector, a model.
data/Raw data, and data your software produces.
analysis/Notebooks that turn data into tables and figures.
paper/The paper, written in Quarto. Figures land in paper/figures/ straight from the analysis.

Write the software, with the agent

Claude Code sits beside the editor in the work tab. Tell it what you want in plain English. It reads the project, proposes changes and asks before it does anything you haven’t allowed. Here I asked it to add a second factor to the simulation.

To open any file yourself, click it in the file list on the left. Right-click in the text for a menu of everyday actions: find a file, search the whole project, show the PDF, show or hide the file list.

Run it, and open the notebook

The analysis tab has a terminal on the left, for running your software and any other command-line tool. On the right it starts your project’s notebooks. Press Enter there, or right-click in the editor and choose Open the analysis notebook, and the notebook opens in its own window beside your workspace, like the PDF preview.

Analyse in a live notebook

Analysis happens in marimo, a free, open-source notebook that runs on your computer. Like any notebook, it mixes notes in plain English, the code for each step, and the results that code produces. Unlike most notebooks, it keeps them in step for you: change a cell and every cell that depends on it runs again, so no table or figure on the page is ever out of date.

Ask the agent, watch the notebook. Because the notebook has its own window, the agent can sit right beside it. Go back to the work tab and ask for the next step in plain English. Each notebook is an ordinary Python file in analysis/, so the agent edits it like any other file, and the open notebook updates as soon as it saves.

Your data and databases

Files. Data files go in data/: CSV, Excel, Parquet, or your existing SPSS and Stata files. Python reads them all; ask the agent to load one into the notebook.

SQL. If you’d rather ask questions in SQL, a notebook cell can be SQL instead of Python. It can query the tables already in the notebook, files in data/ directly (FROM 'data/survey.csv'), or a database. The answer is another table you can search and sort, and the cells after it can use it.

Your own database. To work with a Postgres, MySQL, SQLite or other database, choose Add database connection in the notebook’s left-hand panel, or ask the agent to set it up. The database’s tables then appear in that panel, ready to query. Keep the password out of the notebook: put it in a file called .env in the project folder (for example DB_PASSWORD=…) and have the connection read it from there. marimo loads .env when the notebook starts, and Git ignores it, so the password never ends up in a saved version or on GitHub.

The command line. The terminal in the analysis tab is there for everything else: database tools such as psql or duckdb, cloud tools such as AWS’s aws, and ssh to log in to a university computing cluster.

Try ideas with a slider

Notebooks can be interactive too. Here the agent added a slider for how much stress costs: drag it, and the simulation and the fit run again at once. It’s a quick way to see how sensitive a result is before you commit to an analysis.

Why this beats an output window. In SPSS or Stata the analysis happens in one place, the results pile up in another, and the explanation lives in your head or a separate document. A notebook keeps all three together, in order, so it reads like a lab notebook that keeps itself up to date. Six months later you can see exactly how every number was made. Because each notebook is plain text, the review tab shows every change to it line by line. To run the whole notebook from start to finish, for example after changing the software, type uv run analysis/01-explore.py in the analysis tab’s terminal, or ask the agent to.

Write the paper, with a live PDF

The paper is paper/index.qmd, written in Markdown: plain text with a few marks for headings, emphasis and citations. The paper tab rebuilds the PDF every time you save. To see it, right-click in the text and choose Show PDF preview. The PDF opens in a window beside the editor, and clicking any sentence in it takes the editor to that sentence.

Citations. With Zotero open, type \ci in the paper (press \ then c then i, not in typing mode) and Zotero’s search box appears. Pick a paper and its citation, such as [@knuth1984], appears at the cursor. The reference list builds itself.

Grammar. Spelling and grammar suggestions appear as you write, from a checker called Harper that runs on your own computer.

Read what changed, and talk back

By the time you look, the agent’s changes are already in your files. The review tab shows every line that has changed since you last saved a version, whether you changed it or the agent did. You don’t approve changes one at a time. You read them, and if something looks wrong or raises a question, you say so.

Leave a note on a line. In the review tab, move to the line with j (down) and k (up), press c, type your note and press Ctrl + S to save it. Then tell the agent in the work tab: “I left a comment in Hunk. Reply to it there.” It reads your note and answers underneath it, on the same line. If you ask for a change, it makes it and the review updates. Press a to show or hide the agent’s notes.

Save a version and back it up

When you’re happy with where the project is, save a version of the whole project (a commit) in the git tab. You can also just ask the agent: “commit this with a short description”. The first time, ask it to “create a private GitHub repository for this project and push it”, and your work is backed up from then on.

Round again

This is where keeping everything in one place pays off. Change the simulation and the notebook runs again; the figure it saves into paper/figures/ updates in the PDF preview within seconds. Ask the agent to “check that the numbers in the Results section still match the analysis”, and it can, because it can see the code, the data and the paper together.

Coming back tomorrow

Open the Omarchy menu, choose Projects and pick your project. Everything comes back as you left it: the editor, the agent with your conversation, the console and the PDF builder. Even after you restart the computer, opening the project from the menu starts each tab’s program again.

Part 3Make it yours

Play, build and share

The setup in this guide isn’t finished, and it isn’t meant to be. Every part of it, down to the menus, is text your agent can read and change. That means the most useful software for your research may be software that doesn’t exist yet, and you can ask for it.

Start with “I wish I had a tool that…” and see how far your agent gets. Some ideas:

A small app for coding interview transcripts, with your codebook built in
A page that shows which participants you still need to recruit
A Monday-morning digest of new papers on your topic, straight into Zotero
A script that turns lecture notes into slides and a handout
A checker that flags any number in your paper the analysis doesn’t produce
A new project template for the kind of study you run most

Play with it. Your projects are saved version by version, so a bad experiment is one step to undo, and your agent can usually put back anything it changed on the computer. Most of what’s in this guide started as an afternoon of asking Claude for something I wanted.
